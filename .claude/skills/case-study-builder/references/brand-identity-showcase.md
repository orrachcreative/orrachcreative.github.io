# Brand identity showcase

Use this when a project includes brand identity work: logo, color, typography, patterns, and real-world application. This is optional and only applies when the project actually involved brand-building, not just UI design.

## Section vs standalone page — how to decide

**Embed as a section within a product case study** when the brand work supported a larger product/UX effort — the brand system existed to serve the app, dashboard, or platform, and the product decisions are still the main story (e.g. Afordal: the design system section already covers this, brand assets are supporting evidence, not the headline).

**Treat as its own standalone page/case study** when brand identity IS the primary output of the project — a full rebrand where the deliverable is the identity system itself, not a product (e.g. a company rebrand with logo, signage, vehicle wraps, packaging). If the user has multiple brand-heavy projects, these can share one "brand identity" portfolio page rather than each getting a full narrative case study, since the display format (visual system + applications) repeats well across projects.

If genuinely unsure which fits, ask: is the brand system in service of a product decision the user needs to explain, or is the brand system itself the thing being judged? The former embeds, the latter stands alone.

## What to include

Group into two tiers: the system, then the system in the world.

**The system**
- Logo suite — primary mark, secondary/alternate lockups, icon-only mark, color/mono/reversed variations
- Color palette — swatches with usage notes (primary vs accent vs neutral), not just hex values
- Typography — headline and body faces, weight/size hierarchy
- Patterns or graphic elements — if the identity includes a repeating motif, texture, or iconography system

**The system in the world**
- Real-world application mockups — signage, apparel (hats, uniforms), packaging, vehicle wraps, anything that shows the identity surviving contact with a real physical or environmental context, not just a screen
- A digital application — landing page or website design, showing the identity carried into a digital surface

## How to write it (lighter than a full case study)

A brand showcase is closer to a portfolio gallery than a "working it out" narrative — don't force full decision blocks (problem/first attempt/final direction) onto every color choice, that reads as padding. Instead, give each major piece a short caption-style rationale: one to two sentences on why this mark, why this palette, tied back to something concrete about the brand (audience, industry, positioning), not just aesthetic preference.

If there's a genuine hard call in the brand work — a rejected direction, a real constraint (e.g. a client's existing brand equity, industry expectations, a color that doesn't reproduce well on vehicle wrap material) — that's worth a real "working it out" style callout even on a brand page, since it's the same differentiator as elsewhere: showing a reasoned decision, not just a finished asset.

## Layout notes

Reference pattern: Allan Peters' portfolio (petersdesigncompany.com) — one short intro block, then a long, deliberately sequenced stream of full-bleed images with no section headers or labels breaking it up. No "logo suite" / "color palette" / "typography" boxes. The sequence itself does the storytelling: mark close-up, lockups, the mark applied in an environment, then out into the world (signage, apparel, packaging, vehicle wrap), digital application near the end. Resist the urge to over-label — the images carry the narrative once the intro sets it up.

The intro block is the only real text on the page per project: who the client is, what the problem or opportunity was, what the concept/solution is, in two to three sentences. Don't repeat this reasoning in captions throughout the image stream, that duplicates the intro and slows the scroll down.

If the project was collaborative rather than solo, add a plain credit block right after the intro: role paired with name, one line each. This is a completed practice worth following exactly, since it's an honesty move as much as a stylistic one, never let a team project read as solo work.

Real-world mockups should show context (the sign on an actual building facade, the hat being worn, the wrap on a moving truck) rather than isolated on white — that's part of what's being proven, the identity holds up outside a design file.

A grid-of-labeled-sections layout is a reasonable fallback if the user wants something more scannable/structured than the continuous-stream approach, but default to the Peters-style sequence unless the user prefers otherwise.
